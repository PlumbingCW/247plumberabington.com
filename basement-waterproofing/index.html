Basement waterproofing in Abington covers two different trades. A plumber handles water that can be collected and piped away, which means interior drains, the sump pit and pump, the discharge line, backwater valves and advice on routing downspouts. A waterproofing specialist handles the structure, which means exterior excavation, wall membranes and foundation crack repair.

Large specialist companies dominate local search for this topic, and for structural work they are often the right call. We would rather say that plainly than sell you the wrong fix. Our line is open 24/7 and a dispatcher can tell you which side of the line your problem falls on, with same-day appointments when available.

What a plumber does and what a waterproofing specialist does

The split follows one question. Is the water being managed after it enters, or is it being stopped at the wall?

Plumber's scope

  • Sump pit, pump and battery backup
  • Discharge piping to a legal outlet
  • Interior drain channels that lead to the pit
  • Backwater valves on basement drains
  • Guidance on routing downspouts away from the house
  • Finding supply and drain leaks that mimic seepage

Specialist's scope

  • Exterior excavation and wall membranes
  • Structural crack repair and wall bracing
  • Regrading the yard around the foundation
  • Footing drains dug along the outside of the house
  • Foundation repair after movement
  • Window well replacement or re-cutting

How to work out where your water is coming from

A short check before any call helps the plumber and saves you from the wrong job.

1Note when it shows up

Water only after rain points to groundwater or runoff. Water in dry weather points to a pipe, condensation or a high water table.

2Find where it enters

Check the floor edge, the wall center, a window well and the floor drain. Each location points to a different cause.

3Read the water meter

Turn off everything, then read the meter and read it again after two hours. Aqua Pennsylvania says if the meter changes, you likely have a leak.

4Look outside

Check whether gutters overflow and whether each downspout empties well away from the wall. Soil that slopes toward the house sends water straight at the foundation.

5Photograph and keep a log

Take pictures with dates after each storm. A record of when and where helps whoever fixes it, and your insurer.

Interior drains, backwater valves and window wells

An interior drain is a channel cut along the base of the wall that catches seepage and carries it to the sump pit. It manages water after it has entered, rather than stopping it outside, and it works best in a basement with a sound pump and a clear discharge. The slab is opened along the wall, so the cost depends on how much floor is cut.

A backwater valve, also called a check valve on a drain line, closes when sewage tries to flow backward. The township suggests a check valve on basement drains, which is a practical answer if your floor drain has ever gurgled during a storm.

A French drain buried around the outside of a house is excavation work that most waterproofing companies do. A window well that fills with water may need a drain tied to the pit, or may need to be rebuilt, which is where a plumber and a specialist can both be involved.

Rain, roofs and the rules your discharge must follow

NOAA 1991 to 2020 normals give about 45 inches of precipitation a year at the Philadelphia Northeast Airport station. By simple math, that is roughly 28,000 gallons a year for every 1,000 square feet of roof. Where those downspouts empty decides how much of it your basement meets.

Abington Township asks that downspouts and floor drains stay out of the sanitary sewer, and it says connecting a sump pump there is illegal. Water has to go to the yard, a seepage pit or the storm sewer. Any plan that sends roof water into the sewer, however convenient, should be refused.

Flood history: what plumbing can and cannot do

The township's plan says Abington holds the headwaters of the Tookany, Pennypack and Sandy Run watersheds, and that 76 buildings sit in the one percent annual chance (100-year) floodplain. If your property is near one of those creeks, check the floodplain status with the township before you decide what to buy.

A pump and drain handle seepage and rainfall. They cannot hold back a creek in flood, and the township's own record of the 1996 and 2001 floods shows that limit. After 1996 it bought and demolished 38 homes in the most flood-prone areas, which is the clearest sign that some water is a land problem, not a pipe problem.

Flood damage from a rising creek is usually a separate insurance question from a plumbing failure. Ask your insurer what applies to your address, and ask township staff for your flood map status. The same questions apply on the Jenkintown side of the township line.

What drives the cost of a plumbing-side basement fix

A quote should come with a clear price before work starts, and it should show what is in scope. These are the main drivers.

How much floor is openedAn interior drain needs a trench cut in the slab, and a longer run means more cutting, more debris and more concrete to patch.
Pit, pump and backupA new pit costs more than an existing one. A larger pump, a second pump or a battery backup adds equipment.
The discharge routeA short run to open yard is simple. A long run, a seepage pit or a storm sewer connection takes more labor and may need a permit.
Finishes in the wayA finished basement with drywall, flooring or built-ins adds the work of removing and restoring them.
