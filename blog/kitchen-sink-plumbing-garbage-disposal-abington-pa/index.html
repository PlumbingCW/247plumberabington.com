How Kitchen Sink Plumbing Works With a Garbage Disposal in Abington, PA

Follow the water from the strainer to the wall and you can see where leaks, smells and clogs start. This guide shows what you can fix and what belongs to a plumber.

By Abington 24/7 Plumbing | Published 2026-10-22

Water leaves the sink through a strainer, passes through the disposal, then drops through a short discharge tube into a P-trap. The trap holds a small plug of water that blocks sewer gas, and a trap arm carries the flow into the wall drain, which is vented to the roof. The dishwasher drain hose joins this path at a port on the disposal or at a tailpiece on the sink side.

A plumber's job is to make every joint tight, keep the trap in place, and set the slope so water keeps moving. The dishwasher line should loop high or pass through an air gap so dirty water cannot flow back, in whatever form your plumber installs it to code.

Follow the water from the strainer to the wall

Every kitchen is a little different, but nearly all of them follow the same path. Open the cabinet under the sink and you can trace it.

1Sink flange and strainer

A flange sits in the drain hole and is sealed with putty or a gasket. A mounting assembly underneath locks the disposal to it.

2The disposal itself

A motor spins a plate that throws scraps against a grind ring. It does not use sharp blades like a blender, and it needs running water to flush the pieces along.

3The dishwasher connection

The dishwasher drain hose attaches to a small inlet on the disposal. New units often have that inlet plugged, so the plug has to be knocked out or the dishwasher will not drain.

4Discharge tube and P-trap

A short pipe leaves the disposal and meets the trap. The U-shaped bend keeps a water seal in place that stops sewer gas from rising into the kitchen.

5Trap arm and vent

The trap arm runs into the wall at a slight downward pitch. A vent line connects nearby so air can enter behind the water and keep the drain from gurgling.

Where kitchen sink leaks usually start

Water at the top flange

Drips around the rim of the drain opening often mean the seal under the flange has dried out or the mounting ring has loosened.

Water from the side of the disposal

A wet seam on the disposal body usually means a worn internal seal. Most plumbers replace the unit rather than patch it.

A drip at the dishwasher hose

The clamp on the hose may have loosened, or the hose may have cracked. It is a small fix if caught early.

Seepage at the trap nuts

Slip nuts can loosen after a plunger or a clog clearing. Hand-tightening sometimes stops it, but cracked plastic washers need replacing.

Wet cabinet floor near the supply lines

A weeping shutoff valve or braided supply hose can look like a drain leak. Dry each joint with a paper towel and watch which one wets first.

Try it yourself or call a licensed plumber

Cut power to the disposal at the breaker or unplug it before reaching near it, and never put a hand into the chamber.

Reasonable to try at home

  • Press the reset button on the bottom of the unit
  • Free a jammed plate using the hex socket under the unit if yours has one
  • Hand-tighten a loose slip nut and recheck
  • Run cold water while grinding and for a short time afterward
  • Clear the sink strainer and the dishwasher hose of food debris

Leave to a plumber

  • A leak from the disposal body
  • Moving a trap, adding a vent or changing the drain slope
  • Hardwiring a disposal or replacing its power cord setup
  • Sink water backing up into the dishwasher
  • A drain that clogs again soon after being cleared

What Abington Township says about disposals and kitchen drains

Abington local note

The township code allows ground garbage from domestic garbage grinders of an approved type to enter the sewer, so a disposal itself is not prohibited. The same code bars waste with more than 100 parts per million of fat, oil or grease, and the township wastewater page asks residents to limit disposal use and keep grease out of drains. In practice, a disposal is fine for soft scraps, and grease is where your kitchen line gets in trouble.

The code also says grease interceptors are not required for private living quarters or dwelling units. That means a typical home kitchen does not need one, but it also means your own habits and trap are the only protection against grease buildup. The township owns the sewer main, so a backup into several fixtures when no one is using water is a call to 215-884-8329.

Abington's code lists replacing dishwashers, traps and sinks as residential work that still needs a township permit, and plumbing permits are filed by a master plumber registered with the township. Swapping only a disposal is not on that list in the notes we checked, so ask the township's Code Enforcement office at 267-536-1000, ext. 4, or ask your plumber whether your job needs one.

What not to send down a disposal

Grease, oil, coffee grounds in bulk, potato peels, pasta and rice are common causes of kitchen clogs. They swell, stick or harden in the trap and the wall drain, even when the disposal grinds them fine. Scrape plates into the trash first and use the disposal for small soft scraps.
