Boiler Thermostat Not Working in Abington, PA: What to Check First

A dead or ignored thermostat is often a battery, a setting or a switch. Work through the checks below in order, and stop the moment you smell gas.

By Abington 24/7 Plumbing | Published 2026-10-22

Call 24/7
(215) 714-6692
Call now

When a boiler thermostat stops working, the cause is usually simple. Dead batteries, the wrong mode or setpoint, and a boiler switched off at its own service switch or breaker explain a large share of cold-house calls. Check those three first, and if the display is blank, start with fresh batteries.

If the thermostat looks normal but the boiler never fires, the fault may sit in the wiring or in the boiler itself, and that is a job for a licensed plumber or heating technician. If you smell gas at any point, leave the building right away and call the gas utility or 911 from outside, before you call us or anyone else.

First, make sure you are looking at a boiler

This guide applies if hot water or steam from a boiler heats your radiators or baseboards. Plenty of houses heat with forced air instead, and those thermostats follow different rules. Look in the basement for a boiler with pipes running off it to radiators or baseboard units, or ask whoever services your heat.

The thermostat on the wall is only a switch that tells the boiler when to heat. It does not heat anything itself. That is why a thermostat problem and a boiler problem can look identical from the living room.

The checks to run, from easiest to hardest

Go in this order. Most of these take a few minutes, and none of them requires opening the boiler.

1Look at the display and swap the batteries

A blank screen or a low-battery warning points to batteries. Replace them with fresh ones of the type printed in the battery bay, and give the unit a minute to restart.

2Confirm the mode and setpoint

The thermostat should read Heat, with the target several degrees above the room temperature. Check that a schedule or a hold setting is not overriding what you just set.

3Check that the boiler has power

Most boilers have a service switch, often shaped like a light switch, on or near the unit. Make sure it is on. Then check the breaker at the panel, flipping it fully off and on once if it looks tripped.

4Read the boiler's own light or display

Many boilers show a status light or an error code. Write down exactly what it shows, because that helps the technician. Do not press the reset button over and over, since repeated resets can hide a real fault.

5Look for a pattern

Does the heat come on late, shut off too soon or run all day? A thermostat sitting in direct sun, near a draft or on a cold exterior wall can misread the room and give the boiler the wrong signal.

6Stop and call

If these checks do not bring heat back within a short time, call. Tell the dispatcher what the thermostat shows, what the boiler shows and what you already tried.

Signs the problem is the boiler and not the thermostat

Strange noises

Banging, loud gurgling or a squeal from the boiler room points to the boiler or its pump, not a wall control.

Water around the boiler

Damp floor, drips or rust stains near the unit mean a leak. A boiler that keeps losing pressure may also have a hidden leak somewhere in the heating pipes, and leak detection can help find it.

Error code or lockout light

A steady fault light or a code on the boiler display is the boiler reporting its own problem.

No hot water either

If your boiler also makes the household hot water and both are out, the fault is probably at the boiler. A separate tank may need water heater repair instead.

Soot or a burning smell

Black marks near the unit or a scorched smell are warning signs. Switch off the boiler and call. If you also smell gas, leave first.

Heating controls and permits in Abington

Abington heating rules

Abington's township code lists repair and replacement of control devices for heating and air-conditioning equipment among the residential work that still requires a permit. The township's HVAC permit application also asks for unit specifications and a separate electrical permit application. For you, swapping batteries needs nothing, but if your contractor replaces a control device or rewires it, ask whether the job will be permitted through Code Enforcement at 267-536-1000, extension 4.

PECO's customer handbook says to have your house heater inspected and cleaned at least every two years, and the federal Consumer Product Safety Commission says to put battery-operated or battery-backup carbon monoxide alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas. Treat that as a floor, not a ceiling, and ask your technician how often your unit needs service. A boiler that has been checked is less likely to leave you with a cold house on a January night.

A word on thermostat wiring

Most boiler thermostats work on a low-voltage circuit. The thermostat calls for heat by connecting two small wires at its base, and the boiler responds. A loose, corroded or damaged wire can break that call even when the thermostat itself is fine.

Some baseboard systems use line-voltage thermostats that carry full house current. Those are electrician territory, and a shock risk if you open them. Before you touch any wiring, shut off power at the breaker, photograph the terminals so you can reconnect them, and stop if anything looks burned or brittle.

Do it yourself or call

Fine for a homeowner

  • Replacing thermostat batteries
  • Checking the mode, setpoint and schedule
  • Flipping the boiler service switch or a tripped breaker once
  • Dusting the thermostat and moving a misleading sensor
  • Photographing the boiler's error code

Leave to a licensed pro

  • Opening the boiler or working near the burner
  • Relighting a pilot or touching a gas valve
  • Rewiring or replacing a hardwired control
  • Adding water or changing boiler pressure
  • Repeated lockouts or any smell of gas

Gas smell or carbon monoxide alarm

If you smell gas, leave the building immediately and do not flip switches or use anything electrical on the way out. Call your gas utility or 911 from outside or from a neighbor's phone. Call a plumber only after the utility says the house is safe. If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or anyone has a headache, dizziness, nausea or confusion, get everyone outside and call 911.
