Outdoor Faucet Repair and Freeze Protection in Abington, PA

A hose bib that drips at the handle is often a quick fix. One that leaks inside the wall, or split over winter, needs a plumber. Here is how to tell, and when to shut it off for the cold.

If water drips from the spout while the handle is off, the washer or the valve seat is usually worn, and a repair kit may fix it. If water leaks at the handle stem, tightening the packing nut or replacing the packing often stops it. If water runs inside your wall or basement when you use the hose, the faucet probably froze and split, and it needs replacing.

In Abington, the date that matters is the first fall freeze, which averages near November 7. Disconnect hoses and shut the inside valve before then.

Why a hose bib splits when the weather turns

A standard hose bib is a short pipe through the wall with a valve at the outer end. When you leave a hose attached, water stays in the pipe behind the valve. If that water freezes, it expands and splits the pipe, usually just inside the wall where you cannot see it.

The split often stays hidden until spring. You turn on the hose, the water goes through the crack and not out of the spout, and your basement ceiling or wall gets wet. If you see a damp joist or a stain below the faucet after the first use of the season, shut the inside valve and call.

A frost-free sillcock is built differently. The valve seat sits deep inside the heated part of the house, and the stem runs through a long tube, so there is little water left at the outside end. It still needs the hose removed in winter, and it needs to tilt slightly downward so it can drain.

Common outdoor faucet problems and what they usually mean

Drip from the spout when off

The washer or seat inside the valve is worn. A kit or a new valve cartridge often fixes it, if the body is sound.

Leak around the handle

The packing under the handle has dried out. Snug the packing nut a quarter turn, and replace the packing if that does not stop it.

Water sprays at the wall

A crack or a loose joint behind the faucet is likely. Shut the inside valve now and have it looked at.

Low flow at the hose

A clogged vacuum breaker, a half-closed inside valve or rust inside an old pipe can cut the flow.

Faucet wobbles or rotates

The mounting is loose or the pipe behind it is corroded. Do not crank on it, since that can break the line.

Wet basement wall below the faucet

Water inside the wall, often from a freeze split. Turn off the inside valve and call before the damage spreads.

How to shut down a hose bib for winter

This takes about ten minutes per faucet. Do it before the first cold week, not after.

1Disconnect the hose and any attachments

Take off splitters, timers and nozzles too. A full hose holds water against the faucet and invites a split.

2Find the inside shutoff

In many Abington basements, it sits on the line just inside the wall behind the outdoor faucet. Some faucets have no separate inside valve, and then a plumber may recommend adding one.

3Close the inside valve

Turn it fully off. If the handle is stiff or leaks when you turn it, stop and call, since forcing it can break the stem.

4Open the outside faucet

Leave it open so the trapped water drains. A few drips are normal.

5Drain the inside line if the valve has a bleeder cap

Open the little cap, let the water out into a bucket, and close it again. If there is no cap, leave the outside faucet open.

6Cover only if it is not sealed

A foam cover adds little to a faucet that is already drained. Do not wrap a faucet that still has water in it and think it is protected.

What to try yourself and what to hand off

Reasonable to try at home

  • Replacing a worn washer on a compression-style bib
  • Tightening or repacking a leaking handle stem
  • Cleaning a clogged vacuum breaker
  • Removing hoses and draining the faucet in fall
  • Checking the basement for drips each spring

Call a licensed plumber

  • A faucet that leaks inside the wall
  • Replacing a hose bib with a frost-free sillcock
  • A shutoff that will not close or leaks when turned
  • A faucet on a line you cannot trace
  • Any pipe work in a finished wall or ceiling

Replacing the faucet instead of repairing it

A new faucet makes sense when the body is cracked, the threads are corroded or the line behind it has frozen before. A frost-free model costs more than a basic bib, but it is the version that tends to survive a hard cold snap. It also needs the right length for your wall thickness and a slight slope so it drains.

The plumber will usually cut the old faucet out, check the pipe behind it, and solder or press on a new one with a vacuum breaker. For more on that kind of work, see our page on faucet repair, which covers indoor and outdoor fixtures. If a split pipe is hiding in the wall, a leak detection visit can find the wet area before anyone opens drywall.

Homes near Wyncote and Elkins Park often have older pipe behind the faucet, which is one more reason to look at the line and not just the spout. If you are not sure which type you have, a photo of the wall side in the basement tells the plumber a lot.
