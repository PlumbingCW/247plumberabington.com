Stop valve will not close: the fallback to the main

Work through these in order. Each one is safer than the next.

1Try the fixture stop by hand

Turn the small oval handle clockwise. A quarter-turn ball valve stops at a half turn, and a multi-turn valve may need several rotations.

2Do not force a seized stem

Pliers and wrenches can snap a stem or crack the valve body, which turns a slow drip into an open pipe. If it fights you, leave it and move on.

3Go to the main shutoff

A lever turns a quarter turn across the pipe to close. A round handwheel turns clockwise until it stops. Tell the dispatcher which kind you have.

4Drain the lines

Open the lowest faucet in the house, such as a basement sink or outdoor spigot, to pull water out of the pipes and reduce pressure at the leak.

5Catch and protect

Put a bucket or towels under the leak, empty the cabinet and keep cords away from the wet floor. Photograph the damage before you clean up.

6If the main will not close either

Say so on the phone, because that changes the plan. Aqua owns the meter, so do not operate anything at the meter or curb unless Aqua tells you to. Aqua customer service is 877-987-2782.

Four faucet designs and how each one wears out

Cartridge faucets

A single lever moves a sealed cartridge. When the seals wear, you get a drip from the spout or a lever that feels loose. The fix is usually a new cartridge.

Ceramic disc faucets

Two polished discs slide against each other inside the body. They last a long time, but mineral grit can scratch them and cause a slow drip that a new disc set cures.

Compression faucets

Separate hot and cold handles press a rubber washer onto a seat. A worn washer or a pitted seat causes a drip, and the older the faucet the more likely it is.

Ball faucets

A single handle turns a slotted ball against springs and seals. Leaks usually come from worn seats and springs, or from the O-rings at the base of the spout.

A broken or burst supply valve under the sink

The braided line that feeds a faucet can burst at its coupling, and the stop valve can start leaking from its stem or from the compression nut. Water then runs down the cabinet floor and into the wall behind it. You may notice a damp cabinet back, a hissing sound or a sudden drop in pressure at that one sink.

A plumber will usually shut the main, cut out the failed valve and install a new quarter-turn stop with a fresh braided line. If the copper stub coming out of the wall is crumbling or pitted, that stub has to be replaced too, and a patch on weak pipe tends to leak again. When water has already run into a cabinet floor or wall, ask about a leak detection check to be sure it is not still going somewhere.

If more than one fixture is losing water, or the leak is in the wall, treat it like the burst pipe emergency it may be. Keep the main closed until the plumber says otherwise.

Repair the faucet or replace it

A faucet can be rebuilt many times, but it is not always worth it. The plumber compares the body, the parts and the finish before recommending either.

Repair usually makes sense when

  • The drip comes from the spout or a handle only
  • The body and finish are sound
  • A matching cartridge, disc or washer is available
  • The faucet is fairly recent and has not been repaired before
  • The fixture matches the sink and counter you want to keep

Replace usually makes sense when

  • The body is corroded, pitted or cracked
  • Parts are discontinued or hard to find
  • The same faucet has leaked after earlier repairs
  • Water seeps from the base or under the sink
  • You want a taller spout, a pull-down spray or a touchless model

Outdoor spigots and the first freeze

A spigot that drips only when a hose is attached usually has a worn washer, a failed packing nut or a bad vacuum breaker. A spigot that leaks inside the wall after a cold night may have split behind the siding, where it is hard to see. NOAA normals for the Philadelphia Northeast Airport station put the average first fall freeze near November 7 and the last spring freeze near April 4.

In practice, that means hoses should come off, the interior shutoff to each spigot should close, and the spigot should drain before early November. Hold off on reconnecting hoses until after early April. The Township's code lists replacing bib valves among residential work that can need a permit, so ask Community Development before swapping one.

Shower and tub drains that will not clear

A tub or shower that drains slowly is usually a hair-and-soap clog in the trap, and a drain snake or a clean-out of the stopper often clears it. Chemical openers can damage old fittings, so use them with caution. If the tub, the toilet and a basement drain all gurgle together, the clog has moved to the main line and drain cleaning is the next step.
