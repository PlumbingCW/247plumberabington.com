A sewer camera inspection sends a waterproof camera through the pipe that carries wastewater from your house to the sewer main, and a plumber watches the picture live. The screen usually shows something specific, such as joints that have slipped out of line, tree roots pushing through a seam, a low spot that holds water, hairline cracks or flaking scale inside old cast iron.

The inspection is a diagnosis, not a repair. It tells you whether the line needs cleaning, a spot fix, a liner or a full replacement, or whether it is fine as it is. Call (215) 714-6692 any hour to book, and ask about same-day times.

What the camera sees inside a house sewer line

Offset or separated joints

Where two pieces of pipe no longer line up, debris catches on the lip and a clog forms in the same place again and again.

Root intrusion

Fine roots enter through seams and grow into a mat. A camera shows whether they are a few strands or a mass that fills the pipe.

Bellies or sags

A section that has settled holds standing water, and solids collect there. The camera shows a pool where the pipe should drain.

Cracks and breaks

Longitudinal cracks, collapsed sections and holes let soil in and wastewater out. They tend to get worse with time and ground movement.

Scale and corrosion in cast iron

Old cast iron roughens from the inside, and a layer of scale narrows the pipe until it catches paper and grease.

Grease, debris and foreign objects

Hardened grease, wipes and toys can sit in a pipe that is otherwise sound, and cleaning clears them.

When to book a camera inspection

Four situations make a camera worth the visit. In each, a picture of the line replaces a guess about something that is expensive to get wrong.

  • ✓A clog keeps coming back. If snaking or jetting works for a few weeks and then the drain slows again, something in the pipe is catching debris. The camera shows what.
  • ✓You are buying a house. A line that looks fine from the basement can be cracked or root-filled under the yard. Buyers often add a camera inspection to the checkup, and the video becomes a negotiating fact.
  • ✓You plan to finish a basement. Floor drains, a new bathroom and walls all sit on top of the line. Learning about a problem before the drywall goes up costs far less than finding it after.
  • ✓A backup just happened. A sewage backup tells you the line is blocked or failing. After the line is cleared, a camera shows whether the cause was one-time or structural.

What happens during the visit

1Find a way in

The plumber looks for a cleanout, the capped fitting that gives access to the line. If there is none, a toilet can be pulled or a vent used, and the plumber will tell you what that adds.

2Run the camera

A flexible cable pushes the camera down the line while it records. The plumber watches for changes in pipe material, joints, water and obstructions.

3Locate trouble spots

Many cameras carry a transmitter, so the plumber can trace a problem from above ground and give you its depth and position, where the equipment allows.

4Review the video together

You see what the plumber sees and hear what each finding means for your house. Ask for a copy of the recording.

5Choose the next step

The plumber lays out options with a clear price before any repair work starts. Nothing needs to be decided on the spot.

What each finding means for your next step

A camera finding points to one of five paths. This table shows how plumbers usually match what is on screen to what comes next, though your pipe may need a different answer.

What the camera showsUsual next stepWhy
Clean pipe, sound joints, normal flowNothing to fix. Keep the recording for your records.A clog may be in the house drains instead, see drain cleaning
Grease, paper, wipes or loose debrisClean the line, often with hydro jetting or a cableThe pipe is sound, and the obstruction is the problem
Roots in joints but the pipe is intactCut the roots, then set a cleaning scheduleRoots return, so cutting is maintenance, not a cure
One cracked, offset or sagging sectionSpot repair or a liner, see sewer line repairFixes the failed part without replacing the whole run
Widespread cracks, collapse or heavy scaleReplace the line, by trench or trenchless where possiblePatching a worn line often leads to another failure soon

Who owns what between the house and the main

Abington Township owns and runs its sanitary sewer system, including the mains and the treatment plant on Fitzwatertown Road in Roslyn. The pipe from your house to the main is your lateral, and the township says that if a blockage sits between the home and the main, you should contact a plumber. If only your own drains are slow, the problem is probably inside your pipes.

If sewage is backing up into your house right now, call the township's wastewater line at 215-884-8329, which also takes backup calls. The township can tell you whether the main is the problem. A camera then shows whether the cause is the main, your lateral or something inside the house, and the sewer line cleaning page covers clearing it.

Age, material and what to expect in older Abington streets

The median home in Abington Township was built in 1956, and about 61 percent of homes predate 1960, according to Census estimates for 2020 to 2024. Laterals from that era are often cast iron, clay or other older materials, and each fails in its own way. Cast iron scales, clay joints let roots in, and sections can settle over decades.

Homeowners in Glenside, Jenkintown and the older streets of Abington proper can ask the plumber what material the camera found and how much of the line it could reach. The answer tells you how much life is left, and a worn line is a good reason to plan the work before an emergency forces it.
