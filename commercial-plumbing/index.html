Restaurant grease backups, overflowing restrooms and burst lines after hours

A kitchen drain that will not clear

Grease and food solids build up in kitchen lines, and they often fail during the dinner rush. Water backing up in a floor drain or a three-compartment sink is the usual sign. Stop using the sinks and the dishwasher until the line is cleared.

A restroom that is overflowing

Close the supply stop under the toilet or urinal first. If water keeps rising, the cause is usually a blocked drain, not the fixture. Block off the room so no one tracks water into the hall.

A burst line in an office

A split pipe above a ceiling can drop water on desks and wiring in minutes. Shut off the building main or the floor zone valve, then move electronics. Stay clear of wet ceiling tiles, which can fall.

Sewage or odor from a floor drain

A floor drain that gurgles, smells or brings up water points to a blockage downstream. Keep customers out of the area and do not pour chemicals down the drain. Tell the dispatcher if other drains are slow too.

No hot water in a busy business

A water heater that quit or leaks can close a kitchen or a salon for the day. Turn off its power or gas supply if it is leaking. See the water heater section below for repair and replacement.

While you wait: what the person on site can do

These steps take a few minutes and make the plumber's work faster once someone arrives.

1Find and close the right valve

Start with the stop at the fixture, then the shutoff for that floor or suite, then the building main. If you cannot find or turn a valve, say so on the call.

2Cut power near the water

Switch off breakers for outlets and equipment that are wet or below the leak. Do not step through standing water to reach a panel.

3Contain the water

Use towels, a wet vac or squeegees to keep water away from walls, stock and electrical gear. Stop when it is safe to stop, not when the floor is dry.

4Take photos and notes

Record the time you found the problem, the fixtures affected and any work order number. Photograph the water source and the damage before cleanup.

5Arrange access

Tell the dispatcher who will meet the plumber, where to park and whether a key, code or tenant contact is needed after hours.

How a landlord or property manager call runs, from tenant report to invoice

A managed building adds people to the call: the tenant who sees the problem, the manager who authorizes the work and sometimes an owner who needs a report. This is the order that keeps everyone informed.

1The tenant reports it

A tenant calls you, or you spot the problem on a walkthrough. Get the suite number, what is leaking and whether the water is still running.

2You call the line

Give the building address, the suite, your work order number and who has authority to approve a price. After-hours calls and same-day appointments, when available, follow the same call.

3Access is arranged

Decide who opens the suite, the mechanical room and any roof or basement access. A plumber stuck outside a locked door is a cost nobody wants.

4The price comes before the work

You get a clear price before work starts, and that is the moment to confirm who pays, the tenant, the owner or an insurer. The lease usually settles that, not the plumber.

5The invoice matches the order

Ask that the invoice show the address, suite, date, work done and your work order number. A bill that mirrors the work order is easier to approve and to pass on to an owner.

Commercial drain service for kitchens, offices and retail

Commercial drains carry more load than a house line. A restaurant sends grease and food waste down every shift, and an office restroom may flush hundreds of times a day. The pipes are often larger, which helps, but a blockage also affects more people at once.

A plumber usually starts with a cable machine for soft clogs and roots, then may follow with a camera to see the pipe. For grease that keeps coming back, high-pressure water can scour the pipe walls, see hydro jetting. For routine blockages, our drain cleaning service page explains the tools.

If the blockage sits between your building and the street, the township's role matters. Abington Township owns and runs the public sanitary sewer and takes sewer backup calls at 215-884-8329. Ask the plumber to find out whether the problem is in your line or the main, because that decides who fixes it. Our sewer line cleaning page covers the building side.

Grease trap cleaning: what staff can do and what needs a pro

A grease trap or interceptor catches fats, oil and grease before they reach the sewer. When it is full, grease passes through and builds up in your line. The township's own wastewater page asks residents to keep fats, roots, oil and grease out of the sewer, and a kitchen trap exists for the same reason.

Kitchen staff can do

  • Scrape plates and pans into the trash first
  • Wipe oily cookware with a paper towel before washing
  • Keep drain strainers in place
  • Note how often the trap fills and smells
  • Log each cleaning date

Leave to a plumber or hauler

  • Pumping out and cleaning a full trap
  • Repairing baffles, lids or inlet pipes
  • Clearing grease-packed lines past the trap
  • Camera checks after repeat backups
  • Questions about what your location must follow

Floor drains, lift pumps and ejector pumps

Basements and lower levels in older commercial buildings often sit below the sewer line, so they rely on pumps and drains to move waste up. When one fails, the water has nowhere else to go. The table lists what to watch for.

Floor drainCatches spills and washdown water and sends it to the drain lineA dried-out trap lets sewer odor in, or debris blocks the strainerOdor or slow draining water around the drain
Sewage ejector pumpLifts toilet and restroom waste from a low level up to the sewer lineA jammed float, a worn pump or a stuck check valveGurgling fixtures, an alarm or waste backing up at low levels

The township's sewer FAQ says downspouts, floor drains and sump pumps that add rain or groundwater to sanitary sewers must be removed, so ask the township before you change how any drain is connected. See also sump pump repair.

Backflow testing is a planned job, not an emergency

A backflow preventer is a valve assembly that stops dirty water from flowing backward into the public supply. Many businesses have one on a fire line, a boiler feed, an irrigation system or a commercial kitchen connection. Where one is installed, it is usually tested on a schedule by a certified tester.

We could not confirm a specific rule from Aqua Pennsylvania or Abington Township for every property, so ask Aqua and Community Development what applies to yours. Book the test before an inspection or a lease renewal, not after a failure. If a device fails, repair or replacement is planned work, and a related plumbing installation job may follow.

Restroom fixtures that fail in busy buildings

Public restrooms wear out parts faster than a home bathroom. These are the failures a plumber sees most often.

  • ✓Running or weak flush valves A worn valve wastes water and can make a toilet or urinal flush weakly. It is usually a parts replacement, see toilet repair.
  • ✓Leaking supply stops The small valve under a sink or toilet can drip for months and rot a vanity or floor. Replace it before it fails.
  • ✓Dripping or sticking faucets Sensor and push-style faucets stick, run on or lose pressure. Cartridge or solenoid replacement often fixes it, see faucet repair.
  • ✓Loose or cracked toilets A rocking toilet can break its wax seal and leak into the floor below. Tightening or resetting it takes less time than repairing water damage.
  • ✓Clogs from paper towels and wipes Wipes and paper towels are the most common restroom blockage. A sign on the door helps, but the line still needs clearing when it happens.

Water heaters for businesses

Commercial hot water needs vary a lot. A café needs steady hot water for dishwashing and handwashing. An office may only need a small tank for the break room. The right size depends on how many fixtures run at the same time and how fast the water must recover.

A leaking tank, a pilot that will not stay lit or a heating element that failed are common calls. Our water heater repair and water heater replacement pages explain when to fix and when to replace. For tight spaces or steady demand, a tankless water heater is one option to discuss. Check the vent, gas line and drain pan in the plan, and ask Community Development whether a permit applies.

Commercial leak detection: when it helps and when it does not

A lot of commercial leak calls do not end with a hidden pipe. The water may be a running toilet, a failed fixture, roof or air conditioning condensate, a neighbor's suite or a bill that was estimated. A meter test, with all water off, often points to the answer before any detection equipment is needed.

Leak detection with thermal cameras, moisture meters or acoustic listening is worth paying for when the meter still moves with everything off, or when a wall or ceiling stays wet. If a simple check points elsewhere, the plumber should say so. Our leak detection page explains the methods in detail.

What decides the price of a commercial plumbing call

Every building is different, so a fair price depends on a few things the plumber can only see on site. You get a clear price before work starts, and these are the factors that move it.

Type of workClearing a restroom clog is a short call. A grease-packed line, a failed ejector pump or a pipe behind a finished ceiling takes more time and equipment.
Access and building sizeA locked suite, a roof hatch or a long run to a distant cleanout adds time. A labeled valve map and a contact on site shorten the job.
Parts and equipmentCommercial fixtures, flush valves and pumps are often larger than residential ones. Matching an older model can take a special order.
Time of dayAfter-hours and weekend calls involve a plumber leaving their own time, which can change the rate. The dispatcher tells you before anyone is sent.
