Your meter keeps turning when every tap is closed, the water bill jumped, a patch of floor feels warm or a ceiling stain grows by the hour. Leak detection is the work of finding exactly where water is escaping from a pipe, usually without opening more wall or floor than needed. If water is visible and moving, close the main valve first and then call (215) 714-6692, because the line is answered 24/7.

While you wait, keep outlets and electronics clear of the wet area and photograph the meter dial. Please do not cut into drywall to look for the source. A hole in the wrong place adds repair work and rarely shows the leak.

Six ways a hidden leak shows itself

The meter moves with every fixture off

A dial or flow triangle that turns while nobody is using water is the clearest sign. The next section shows the check that Aqua itself recommends.

A bill that climbs for no reason

If the household did not change and the bill did, a slow leak or a toilet that keeps refilling is a likely cause.

A warm patch on the floor

Warmth under tile, vinyl or carpet often means a hot water line is leaking beneath it, and the heat travels along the slab or subfloor.

A ceiling stain or bubbling paint

Brown rings, sagging drywall and blistered paint mean water has been present for a while, and the source may sit several feet away.

A hiss, drip or trickle inside a wall

Running water sounds are easiest to hear at night. A steady hiss usually means a pressurized line, while a drip may be a joint or a drain.

A soggy strip of lawn or a sudden pressure drop

Wet ground in dry weather, or weak flow at every tap, can point to the pipe between the street and the house.

Water meter and high bill: the two-hour check

Start at the water meter, because it measures every drop that enters the house. Aqua Pennsylvania's customer guidance says to choose a two-hour window with no water use, read the meter before and after, and treat any change as a likely leak. The check costs nothing and takes a few minutes of your time.

1Stop every use of water

Close all taps, pause the dishwasher and washer, and check that the ice maker and humidifier are not filling.

2Read and photograph the meter

Write down the digits or snap a photo with the time. Many meters also show a small triangle or wheel that spins whenever water moves.

3Wait two hours, then read it again

A changed reading means water went somewhere. If the triangle is spinning, you do not even need to wait.

4Close the main valve and look again

If the dial stops, the leak is usually inside the house. If it keeps moving, the leak is often on the buried stretch between the meter and the main valve, covered further down this page.

Aqua does not offer leak adjustments in Pennsylvania

Aqua's leak adjustment page lists several other states and says plainly that it does not offer leak adjustments in PA. A leak that runs for a whole billing cycle is therefore a cost the household carries, which is the strongest reason to find one early. For questions about your meter or your account, Aqua customer service is at 877-987-2782, and the meter itself belongs to Aqua even though the plumbing in an outdoor meter pit is the customer's.

Ceiling and wall leaks: finding the source behind the stain

A stain is where water came out, not where it started. Water follows joists, pipes and the back of drywall before it soaks through, so a ceiling spot can sit several feet from the failed joint. A stain that grows when nobody has used the bathroom points toward a pressurized supply line. One that appears only after a shower points toward a drain, a tub seal or a shower pan.

A plumber usually begins with a moisture meter, which reads dampness through the surface, and may add a thermal camera that shows cooler or warmer patches behind drywall. Listening gear can pick up the sound of a pinhole spray inside a cavity. The aim is a single small access hole in the right place, which is cheaper to patch than a wall opened at random.

Not every ceiling stain is a plumbing leak. Roof leaks, condensation on cold ductwork and clogged air conditioner drain lines look much the same, so the plumber will often shut the water off and watch whether the stain stops growing. If you see water actively spraying, treat it as a burst pipe and shut off the main before anything else.

Under the house and in the crawl space

Most older houses here have basements, but additions, porches and some newer sections sit over crawl spaces. Leaks in those spaces can run for months because nobody goes down to look. Signs include a musty smell, sagging insulation, soft subfloor, mold on joists and a floor that feels spongy near a bathroom or kitchen.

The plumber will usually inspect supply lines, drain connections and the underside of tubs and toilets by flashlight, then pressure test the supply side if no wet joint is visible. Supply lines made of galvanized steel or early copper often fail at fittings. When several joints are weeping, the honest conversation is about replacing the run rather than patching each one, and the repiping page explains that path.

Underground and buried line leaks

The pipe that brings water from the street runs underground for tens of feet, and a leak there can go unnoticed for a long time. The tell-tale signs are a wet or unusually green strip of lawn, a soft spot in the driveway, a meter that keeps moving after the main valve is closed, or a sound of running water where the line enters the basement.

Ownership matters here. Aqua's published guidance says the company owns the line from its main to the curb, and the customer owns and maintains the portion from the curb stop to the building. A leak on the street side belongs to Aqua, so call 877-987-2782 after you confirm where the wet ground is. A leak on the house side is yours to fix, and the full process is on the water service line repair page.

Acoustic listening on the exposed pipe and on the ground above it, plus a pressure test of the line, can narrow the leak to a few feet. That matters because digging the whole yard is expensive and slow. Pennsylvania law asks anyone digging to call 811 at least three business days ahead so buried utilities are marked.

How plumbers find leaks without guesswork

No single tool solves every case, so the method depends on where the water is and what the pipe is made of. These are the common ones, and a plumber will pick from them based on what is on site.

MethodWhat it helps findWhere it falls short
Meter and pressure testWhether a leak exists and whether it is on the supply sideDoes not say where the leak is, only that it is there
Acoustic listeningPressurized leaks in walls, slabs and buried lines by their soundNoisy plumbing or plastic pipe can muffle the signal

Slab leaks

A slab leak is a leak in a pipe that runs in or under a concrete floor. It is less common in houses with full basements but turns up in additions, garages and homes built on slabs. The warning signs are a warm spot on the floor, the sound of running water with no fixture open, a meter that moves, and damp carpet or floor tile that lifts at the edges.

Detection usually combines a pressure test on the hot and cold lines separately, acoustic listening and a thermal scan of the floor. Once the leak is found, the options are breaking the slab at that spot, rerouting the line through walls or the attic, or replacing the line. Rerouting costs more now but avoids reopening the floor, and a worn line with repeated leaks often leans toward a new run.

Housing age and why leaks hide in Abington walls

The median home in Abington Township was built in 1956, and about 61 percent of the 23,232 homes predate 1960, according to Census American Community Survey estimates for 2020 to 2024. Houses that old often combine original galvanized pipe, early copper with soldered joints and later repairs from several eras. When one joint leaks, the pipe beside it is often the same age.

That is why a good leak call ends with a question about the pipe on both sides of the fix. Older streets around Glenside and Jenkintown share this vintage, so the advice applies there too. Ask what material was found, how far the plumber looked in each direction and whether the nearby pipe shows the same wear.

What to do when a leak is an emergency: water on the floor tonight

An active leak that soaks a floor or drips toward a panel is a same-day call whenever the line is open, and it is open at night and on weekends. A slow hidden leak can wait for the next available appointment, but not for weeks. These steps keep the damage down in the meantime.

1Shut off the main if water is visible

The main valve usually sits where the line enters the house, near the meter. Turn it clockwise until it stops.

2Photograph the meter dial

A photo with the time gives the plumber a baseline and shows how fast the water is moving.

3Keep power away from water

If water is dripping toward an outlet, light or panel, stay clear and tell the dispatcher on the call.

4Dry what you can reach

Move rugs, furniture and electronics off wet floors and soak up standing water so it does not travel.

5Note when it started

A date for the first stain, sound or bill change helps the plumber work out whether the leak is old or new.

Sprinklers, pools and the box at the curb

Irrigation systems leak at valves, heads and buried joints, and the signs are a soggy lawn after a watering cycle or a meter that moves when the zone runs. Shut the system off at the controller and check whether the meter stops. If it does, the leak is on the irrigation side, which the plumber can locate by testing one zone at a time.

Pools and spas lose water through returns, skimmer lines and buried plumbing, so the first step is to separate normal evaporation from a real leak. A bucket test or a pressure test on the lines will usually tell the difference. Leaks at the meter box or curb stop show up as a wet patch or standing water in the box itself, and the owner of the leaking part decides who fixes it.
