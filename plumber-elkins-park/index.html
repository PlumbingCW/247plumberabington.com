Before a plumber reaches your door

These moves cost nothing and cut the damage while you wait. Do only what you can do safely.

1Shut off the main

The main valve is usually where the water line enters the house, often near the meter. Turn a round handle clockwise until it stops, or turn a lever a quarter turn. If it will not move, tell the dispatcher.

2Keep power away from water

If water is near an outlet, a panel or a lit appliance, stay back and do not touch them. Switch off the breaker only if you can reach it on dry ground.

3Stop sending water down the drains

If a floor drain or tub is backing up, do not flush, run the washer or use the dishwasher. Every gallon you add may end up on the floor.

4Clear the area

Lift rugs, boxes and electronics off wet floors and photograph the damage first. Your insurer will ask for those photos.

5Smell gas? Leave first

If you smell gas, walk out of the building and call the gas utility or 911 before you call us. A plumber cannot work in a house with a gas leak.

6Call and stay reachable

Give the dispatcher your street address, where the water is coming from and a phone that will pick up. A clear description helps send the right plumber with the right parts.

Signs your house is taking on water or sewage

A floor drain that gurgles in the rain

Bubbling or a rising smell during a storm often means the sewer line is overloaded downstream. Call Aqua's sewer line and ask us to check your own lateral.

A sump pump that never shuts off

A pump that runs for hours after the rain stops is either fighting high groundwater or has a stuck float. Either way it is close to failing.

Damp walls days after the storm

Check the meter. If it keeps moving with every fixture off, the cause is a supply leak and leak detection is the next step.

Slow drains in more than one fixture

A sink, tub and washer all draining slowly points toward the main drain line rather than one trap. Drain cleaning usually starts at the cleanout.

Rust-colored water or weak pressure

In an older house this often points to corroded pipe, especially after a repair elsewhere in the house changed the flow.

What a 1953 median house means for your pipes

Census American Community Survey estimates for 2020 to 2024 put the median Elkins Park home at 1953. Of about 3,139 housing units, roughly 35 percent were built in 1939 or earlier and about 62 percent before 1960. Those are estimates with margins of error, but they tell you what is likely behind your walls.

The oldest houses may still have galvanized steel supply pipe that rusts from the inside, or cast iron and clay drain lines. About 38 percent of units are newer than 1959, so repairs there more often involve copper and plastic. Ask the plumber to name the material on both sides of any repair. A pattern of leaks along one line is the time to ask about repiping.

About 20 percent of Elkins Park units sit in buildings of 50 or more units. In an apartment building the question is which shutoff isolates which riser, and a property manager should know before the emergency. Our commercial plumbing page covers building-scale calls.

Handle it yourself or hand it off during a flood

A wet basement tempts people to start fixing things. Some of that helps and some of it risks a shock or a bigger bill.

Reasonable to do yourself

  • Close the main water valve
  • Move belongings off the floor
  • Photograph every wet surface
  • Check that the sump discharge hose is still attached
  • Mop standing water away from outlets

Leave to a licensed plumber

  • Anything near a wet electrical panel
  • Cutting out or replacing pipe
  • Clearing the main line beyond the cleanout
  • Digging to reach a buried line, which needs a PA One Call to 811 at least three business days ahead
  • Work on a gas-fired water heater
