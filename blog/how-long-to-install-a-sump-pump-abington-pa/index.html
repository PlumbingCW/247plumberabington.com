How Long Does It Take to Install a Sump Pump in Abington, PA?

Swapping a pump in an existing pit often takes a few hours. Cutting a new pit in the slab usually takes longer. The house sets the clock.

By Abington 24/7 Plumbing | Published 2026-10-22

Call 24/7
(215) 714-6692
Call now

Replacing a pump in a working pit is often a half-day job, depending on the pipe and the check valve. A first-time install means breaking the basement slab, digging the pit and running a discharge line outdoors, and that can stretch to a full day or more. We cannot promise a duration before seeing the basement.

In Abington Township, the water has to be piped outside the house and never into the sanitary sewer. That rule shapes the job as much as the pump does.

What happens on install day, in order

The order below is for a new pit. A swap into an existing pit skips the first few steps.

1Walk the basement and pick the low point

The plumber looks at where water collects, where the floor slopes and where a discharge pipe can exit. The best pit location is the lowest corner that still has a clear path to an outside wall.

2Cut the slab and dig the pit

A saw opens a square in the concrete, and the plumber digs out soil and stone. Dust and noise are part of this step, and it can be the slowest part of the day.

3Set the basin

A plastic or fiberglass basin goes in, with gravel around it and a lid on top. The top should sit flush with the floor so nothing catches.

4Install the pump and check valve

The pump sits on a firm base, with a float switch that has room to rise and fall. A check valve on the discharge keeps water from flowing back into the pit.

5Run the discharge line outside

A rigid pipe leaves through the rim joist or wall, and ends at a point that sends water away from the foundation. Township guidance says the line must be permanently piped out of the house, to the yard, a seepage pit or a storm sewer.

6Patch the slab and test

The concrete around the basin gets patched, and the plumber pours water into the pit to watch the float lift, the pump run and the water exit. A good test checks that the pump turns off when the water drops.

Abington Township wastewater page and sewer ordinance

Abington note: where the water has to go

Abington Township wastewater page and sewer ordinance

Abington Township's wastewater page says sump pumps must be permanently piped out of the house to the yard, a seepage pit or a storm sewer. The township code also bars sump pump and downspout water from the sanitary sewer, and calls for rigid piping. If an old pump drains into a floor drain or a basement sewer line, the plumber has to re-route it, and that adds time.

The township also says that a connection that sends sump water to the sanitary sewer is illegal, and the code allows fines for violations. For you, that means asking the installer where the discharge will end, and having the answer before work begins. A plumber who plans to tie it into a drain is not following the rule.

If the discharge point is near a property line or will add paved area, the township's stormwater rules may apply. The Engineering office handles those questions. No sump pump permit shows up on the township's permit list, but ask Community Development to be sure about your plan.

Planned install versus emergency replacement

Timing feels different when the basement already has water on the floor.

A planned install

  • Time to pick the pit location and discharge route
  • Room to schedule concrete work and cleanup
  • Chance to add a battery backup or alarm
  • A dry basement while the pit is cut
  • Choice of a day with dry weather

An emergency replacement

  • Water to pump out before work can start
  • A quick swap of the pump in the existing pit
  • Little time to redesign the discharge
  • Cleanup and drying handled separately
  • Larger upgrades saved for a later visit

Why a sump pump matters in an older Abington basement

The township's own planning documents say flooding is the most common natural hazard in Montgomery County and has been a serious problem in Abington. They name floods in 1931, 1964, 1996 and 2001 in the creek basins that touch the township, and they place the Sandy Run floodplain through Crestmont and Roslyn. Abington holds the headwaters of three watersheds, so heavy rain moves through quickly.

None of that tells you whether your basement floods. It does suggest that if your house sits near one of those creeks, a pump is worth thinking about before the next storm, not during it.

If your home is closer to Willow Grove, the Sandy Run side is the one to ask your installer about. A basement that stays damp but never floods may call for basement waterproofing or drainage work, not just a pump. We also dispatch to Elkins Park, where the same questions about discharge and older basements come up.

What to have ready before the plumber arrives

A little prep shortens the day.

  • ✓Clear a path Move boxes and furniture from the corner where the pit will go and from the route to the door.
  • ✓Know your electrical outlet The pump needs a grounded outlet, and a protected circuit is a better choice. Point out where it is.
  • ✓Note where water collects Tell the plumber which corner stays wet after rain and which stays dry.
  • ✓Mention any past water If the basement flooded after a storm or a sewer backup, say so. It may add a backwater valve to the conversation.
  • ✓Ask about the discharge exit Walk the outside wall with the plumber and agree where the pipe will end.

Backwater valves are a separate question

A sump pump handles groundwater, not sewage. The township code requires a backwater valve where basement plumbing is installed or altered, or where a basement could flood with sewage if the public sewer surcharges. Ask your plumber whether your basement fixtures need one. For existing pump trouble, see sump pump repair.
